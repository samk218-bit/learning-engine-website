An airbag can inflate in a fraction of a second. It looks almost magical, but the process is a carefully timed chain of sensing, decision-making and controlled inflation.

## 1. Sensors detect a severe crash

Cars use accelerometers and other crash sensors to measure rapid changes in motion. The airbag control unit checks the signal and decides whether the crash pattern is severe enough to require deployment. A small bump does not automatically trigger every airbag.

## 2. The control unit makes a decision

The controller evaluates sensor information in milliseconds. If the crash meets the system's deployment criteria, it sends an electrical signal to the relevant inflator.

## 3. The inflator fills the bag

Depending on the design, an inflator uses a gas-generating reaction or stored compressed gas to fill the folded fabric bag very quickly. Modern systems are engineered to control the timing and rate of inflation.

## 4. The airbag cushions the occupant

The bag creates a broad, softer surface that helps spread forces over a larger area and reduces contact with hard parts of the vehicle. Vents let gas escape as the occupant moves into the bag, allowing it to cushion rather than act like a rigid balloon.

## Why is timing so important?

The airbag must deploy during the crash sequence, not before it and not after the occupant has already moved too far forward. Seat belts remain essential: airbags are designed to work with them, not replace them.

**The key idea:** sensors identify a dangerous crash, a controller makes a rapid decision, and an inflator fills the bag so it can help manage impact forces.
